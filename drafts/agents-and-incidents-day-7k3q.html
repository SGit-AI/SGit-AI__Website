Agents and Incidents Evening

One evening in central London, two working sessions, no presentations. You bring an agent you actually run. You leave with a behaviour policy for it, written and signed at your table, a debrief and runbook from an incident your table decided, and an encrypted vault holding both. The first thirty seats are free and each carries about £1,500 of value; every seat after that is paid; a sponsor covers the room.

The messaging

Written from the participant's side first. The sponsor and the organiser lines follow from it.

Bring the agent you already run. Leave with the policy that controls it, the incident it could cause, and what you decided.the one-line pitch
Two working sessions, one evening, no slides. Run an incident as a company with no agent policy. Write the policy. Run it again. See what changed.the second sentence, for the invitation
The first thirty seats are free and each is worth about £1,500: a behaviour policy written and signed at your table, and a facilitated incident debrief with its runbook, both in an encrypted vault only you can open.the offer, for the registration page
Everything made in the room is published that night under CC BY. You are the authors.the summit line
One room sponsor covers the evening, in cash or as the venue with food and drink. Your name is on the night and on every outcome, and you are the reason thirty people got in free.the sponsor ask
Thirty people in a room in London who already run agents on real mail, real code and real customers, writing the policies the rest of the industry is still talking about.the line for a partner who brings an audience

On the £1,500: it is honest for either side alone. Adding both sides makes a seat closer to £3,000, which is a stronger line but needs Jemma's number pinned first. Say one or the other, not both.

What happens at a table

Four to six tables, each one kind of agent, each with a facilitator from the community and a scribe. A participant picks a table at the door and stays at it for both sessions, because the before and after is the point.

Session one, 75 minutes. The facilitator runs the table's DECID:R scenario as an organisation that has given its agent the reach the connector gives it and has written nothing down. Six decision points, three options each, a timer, the live metrics on the room display. Thirty minutes. Then the table writes the behaviour policy for that agent in RiskMandate's four parts: the reach, measured from what the connector actually allows; the mandate, in the owner's words; the gap, in both directions; and for each row of the gap, the barrier, typed honestly as a boundary, a setting, an expectation or nothing. Forty-five minutes, with the facilitator correcting as the table goes, and the scribe writing it into the table's vault.

Session two, 60 minutes. The same scenario, as the organisation that now has the policy. The facilitator keeps both decision traces and both sets of metrics side by side. The last fifteen minutes are the table's own debrief: which decisions the policy removed, which it made cheaper, and which it made no difference to. That last list is the honest one and the one worth publishing.

Readout, 15 minutes. One facilitator per table, two minutes each, standing: the pair of traces on the display, the one row of the policy that mattered most, the one that did nothing. The scribes push the vaults. The outcomes page is live before the drinks.

Why £10,000 for the room: central London workshop rooms for forty to eighty list at roughly £850 to £1,500 for the hire, and quality rooms with full catering run toward £150 a head, so forty to sixty people with food, drink, print and the shared display is a £6,000 to £10,000 evening. Operational plan

Eight weeks from the day this is agreed. The first two cost nothing but conversations. The date is fixed when the room is.

Week 0Agree and name it. Evening, two sessions, £10,000 room, 50/50: agreed on the call. Still open: the name, the £1,500 or £3,000 line, four tables or six, two candidate dates.
Week 1Open the first thirty. Publish the page on both sites with the registration writing to the event vault's append lane; no form service, the vault is the list. Announce to the summit community, RiskMandate's early-access members and DECID:R's customers.
Weeks 1 to 3Find the room. One ask, two forms: £10,000 or the venue with food and drink. A company with a floor in central London and a reason to be seen hosting this is the easier yes. The date is fixed the day they say it.
Weeks 2 to 4Recruit facilitators and scribes, one pair per table, from the summit's track leaders and the two communities. One briefing call, one written brief per table.
Weeks 3 to 5Write the scenarios. Each in DECID:R's shape with a matching policy worksheet. Dinis drafts the agent and the policy side, Jemma the incident side, one remote rehearsal per table.
Weeks 4 to 7Sell the paid seats and the supporter slots, directly and through audience partners, with the price set at week 4 from the free-seat take-up.
Week 7Provision. One vault per registered participant, pre-seeded with their table's worksheet and the agenda; read key sent the week before, write key handed over at the door. Print the paper tables' cards.
Week 8The evening. Outcomes published before the drinks. Within the week: one write-up on each of the three sites with the before-and-after pairs as the headline, and the question of when the next one is.

Roles on the night

Two hosts

Dinis and Jemma run the open and the readout, standing, and float. Neither facilitates a table.

A facilitator per table

From the community. Runs the two DECID:R runs and the policy worksheet, and gives the two-minute readout.

A scribe per table

Writes the policy, the decisions and the debrief into the table's vault as they happen, so publishing is a push.

One operator

The display, the vaults, the append lane, the write keys at the door, and the printed fallbacks.

What goes wrong

  • No room sponsor by week 3. Take the cheapest adequate room on the hire fee alone, skip the food, and run it at forty seats. The first thirty stay free; that promise is the reputation.
  • A table has nobody. Collapse to the four core tables at the door.
  • Wi-Fi fails. Every laptop table has the paper version. Only the shared display needs the network, and the operator has the scenarios exported.
  • Someone brings a real incident. Good. The board table takes it after the readout, unrecorded, and nothing from it is published.

Still to decide

  1. The name. Working title: Agents and Incidents Evening. Alternatives: Control and Respond, The Gap, Live, or Open Security Summit London, agents edition, which borrows the pedigree directly.
  2. The value line. "About £1,500 of value" is true of either side alone; "about £3,000" is the two together once Jemma's figure is pinned. Pick one for the public page.
  3. Four tables or six for the first one. The plan says four at thirty to forty people.
  4. Two candidate dates. Eight weeks from an early-October decision lands at the end of November; the alternative is late January. Let the room decide between them.
  5. Who writes to whom first for the room, with this page attached.
