These six were written over two days and none of them sets out to be a series. Read together they make one argument: deciding well needs more than a yes or a no, and stopping well needs a perspective that has to be designed in, for agents even more than for people.

[Agency is not a yes](/articles/agency-is-not-a-yes.html) is the frame: seven things a decider needs, from options beyond yes to authority over the system that produced the request, and a scale on which the weakest of them caps the decision. [Where is the why?](/articles/where-is-the-why.html) is the same problem met in a permission prompt with no reason on it.

[Knowing when to stop](/articles/knowing-when-to-stop.html) starts from people: security champions who had automated away whole classes of bug, then found themselves debating whether GUIDs were random enough. Agents have it worse, because their range is the point. [Who are you protecting against?](/articles/who-are-you-protecting-against.html) gives one way to stop: name the attacker before deciding how secure to be. [Every mistake added a rule](/articles/every-mistake-added-a-rule.html) shows what happens without one: each mistake adds a rule, the rules become the complexity, and the way back is shipping small components instead.

[Hope or enforcement](/articles/hope-or-enforcement.html) is where the argument is tested: one customer service agent, three designs, and a count of which rules a model is only hoped to keep. The desk's [thread note](/articles/desk/range-is-the-feature-so-the-stop-is-designed.html) pulls out the line each of them shares.
