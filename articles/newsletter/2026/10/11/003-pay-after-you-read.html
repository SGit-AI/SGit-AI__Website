SGit Newsroom · Issue 3 · 2026-10-11

Pay after you read, no server by design, and authority that sits outside the model

By Dinis Cruz, written with the Journalist

Abstract: Three days and twenty-four articles. The reading meter went from an experiment to a working model: you pay for the share of a page you read, you say how useful it was and that sets the price, your balance can go below zero without a word of nagging, and your reading builds personas that are yours. Underneath it, the whole newsroom now runs in the reader's browser, with a language model arriving in the tab as the last piece. And from the other side of the network, an answer to Satya Nadella on models as insider risks, and Microsoft's new agent containers mapped to the behaviour policies they make enforceable.

What would you pay for an article, if you only paid after you had read it, and only as much as it was worth to you? Most of what was published between 9 and 11 October is the answer to that question, built and tested one release at a time, and the infrastructure that let it be built in an afternoon. The rest is the same idea from the agents' side: a limit is only a limit if the thing it limits cannot change it.

Pay after you read

The reading meter started on 10 October as a demonstration, a few pence a page from five pounds of credit kept in the browser. By the end of the afternoon it was something else. Pay after you read tells how, in seven releases in under four hours, each one coming from using the one before:

You pay after you see the value.

You pay for the share of a page you scroll through, not for opening it. At the foot of each article you say how useful it was, and that sets the price, from free to double. Your balance can go below zero and stay there; nothing is blocked and nothing nags. Going live with the reading meter wrote down the business case, the numbers we expect and the ones that would prove us wrong, before the first payment:

And going below zero is not a failure state.

The payment link is now live on newsroom.sgit.ai, so the eight weeks have started, and the hypotheses will be reviewed on 6 December. Who will game the reading meter is the security side: eight kinds of reader, none of them attackers, nine ways to cheat a meter kept in the browser, and why every one of them is allowed. Its sharpest line is about agents, which move between kinds of reader in a single request:

An agent is not a step; it is a lift.

Your reading, kept by you

The meter keeps a history because a ledger has to, and that history builds a persona: a name, a front page of its own, and a graph you can watch grow. Pay to keep your persona argues that this, not guilt, is what a reader would pay for: time, context, focus, and a persona that follows you between devices.

The reader should pay because paying helps them.

A link to a persona is the next step, written as a brief: personas kept in a vault by the newsroom's own agents, so that a link can open the newsroom as a CISO, a data protection officer or one particular person, and the reader can follow the persona or make it their own.

The better move is for the person who sends the link to choose it

No server, by design

None of that runs on a server. No server, by design explains the choice: first the database was retired, the file system took its place, and now the back end is retired too. What is left on the server side is commodity, static hosting and a vault server that stores ciphertext it cannot read:

It is no servers of mine with logic in them.

A model in the tab is about the hardest remaining piece. Chrome now ships a language model that any page can prompt, offline, on capable desktops. It is real and it has rough edges, and the objections to it are serious; the way through is to keep the meaning where it belongs:

The way to build on it without being captured by it is to keep the meaning in the data, not in the prompt.

Two pieces underneath it all were also written up: where the vault keys live, on how a key travels sealed to a registry instead of through a chat, and RFC 0001, a request for comments on two key pairs so that a reader cannot write.

The agent is the reader

The newsroom's articles are long, so one article, five readers sends five agents through each one after it is written: a librarian, a cartographer, a historian, an explainer and a storyteller. The agent is the reader does the arithmetic on what that produces. A curated graph answers a question about a concept in a median of 2,579 tokens, against 25,698 to read the five articles:

If a publisher saves an agent more tokens than it charges, paying is rational.

Two more pieces on making things with agents: how to run synthetic users, five invented readers driving a real browser, which found bugs every test had passed over; and the infographic bake-off, every image model on OpenRouter given eleven briefs, 101 images judged blind, for $10.44.

Authority outside the model

Satya Nadella published seven principles for treating models as insider risks. Authority outside the model answers them one by one, with what we run, what is only designed and what is argued, and names five things the list leaves out. Its core is the test this network has used for a while:

a control bounds a grant only if it is enforced by something the grant does not include.

The same argument, in our words translates his post idea by idea into the vocabulary of behaviour policies, and finds the same ending read from the other side:

the more you can constrain an agent, the more you can trust it, and the more autonomy you can afford to give it.

Where the platform draws the line reads Microsoft's new execution containers and agent control specification field by field. The platform bounds what it can see, files, addresses and processes, and the business logic is above that line. That is the shared responsibility model again, and rows of a behaviour policy that were hope can now become boundaries:

an agent must not be the one that decides its own limits.

Closer to home, a second reader the agent cannot skip is a hook that makes a second model approve every email draft, failing closed, after two went out in my voice despite a written rule; re-anchoring prints an agent's rules back after each summary of a long session; and how I work with Claude is the practical guide behind all of it.

sgit.ai

sgit is git for encrypted vaults, clone, commit, branch and merge files that are encrypted with AES-256-GCM before they leave your machine. Open source, Apache-2.0, in beta and powering production workflows.

🔒 Thirty vaults on this site open in your browser with published read keys, every byte decrypted client-side, none of it readable by the server that stores it. The pages that describe them are ordinary static files. The medium is the message.
