## In short

- **Nobody should pay out of guilt.** Paying because you have to, because you feel you should, or because it is the right thing to do works to a degree. It does not scale, and it is not what we want readers to feel.
- **Pay for what it does for you.** Time, context, focus, the ability to make good decisions, a streamlined way through, a better experience.
- **That thing is a persona.** Your reading builds one, with a name and a graph you can watch grow. Keep articles in it or put them out of it, and it changes.
- **You are more than one reader.** Add personas, one per kind of reading. Five are ready to start from: the founder, the journalist, the security lead, the AI builder, the board member. A persona is a way to manage focus.
- **Today a persona lives in one browser.** Open the site on an iPad, then on a laptop, and you are two strangers. That is not a cheat to worry about. It is a bad experience.
- **What people will pay for** is a persona that follows them, to their phone, their laptop and their agent, with the privacy intact.

## Three reasons readers are asked to pay, and why none of them is ours

There are three common reasons a reader pays for what they read, and most reader-funded publishing uses one of them.

**Because they have to.** The paywall. The Guardian's app, Press Gazette reported in September 2025, "sits within a paywall (free users can only read 20 articles per month)".

**Because they feel they have to.** The ask, the count of what you have read, the reminder that others pay. The Guardian's middle tier, at £12 a month, buys among other things the right to "avoid intrusive pop-up supporter messages". The feeling of being asked has a price, and the price is paid to make it stop.

**Because it is the right thing to do.** The donation. Wikipedia is the best example there is: the Wikimedia Foundation reports "over 8 million donors giving an average donation of $10.58" in its 2023-24 financial year. That is a remarkable achievement, and it is what one of the most visited sites in the world reaches by asking everybody, every year.

All three work, to a degree. My view, after trying versions of them, is that they do not scale for anyone who is not already enormous, and that they make the reader feel the wrong thing. "We don't want people to pay for content because they have to, because they feel they have to, because you shame them, because they feel they have a moral obligation." The reader should pay because paying helps them.

## What a reader is actually buying

Not the article. The article is free to read here, and it is free to read with the meter below zero. What a reader is buying, on this site and on most good ones, is:

- **time**, because they reach the thing worth reading sooner;
- **context**, because each piece arrives connected to what they already know;
- **focus**, because the site shows them their kind of reading and not everything;
- **the ability to make good decisions**, because the evidence comes with the argument;
- **a streamlined way through**, because what they have read, kept and dismissed is remembered;
- **a better experience**, because the site knows them back.

Each of those depends on the same asset: a record of what the reader read, how much of it, what they kept and what they rejected, organised so that the site can act on it. The [reading meter](../articles/going-live-with-the-reading-meter.md) already keeps that record, because a ledger has to. Since today it does something with it that the reader can see.

## The persona

Your reading builds a persona. It starts empty, and every page you read adds to it, weighted by how far down the page you read. It gets a name from the topics you read most, until you give it one of your own: read mostly about agents and policy and it is *The Policy Architect*; add graphs and it becomes *The Policy Architect, with a streak of the Cartographer*. It has a front page, [your newsroom](../account/newsroom.md), with the articles it would want next. And it has a graph: you in the middle, the topics you read around you, the pages behind each topic, and the citations between those pages.

Your newsroom, as one of the five starting personas: who it is, what it would read next, with a Keep and a "Not for this persona" on every pick.

You can steer it. Every pick in your newsroom has *Keep* and *Not for this persona*, and every article page has *Keep in your persona* at its foot. A kept article pulls its topics and tags up; a rejected one pushes them down and disappears from the picks. The graph moves when you do. This is the part that matters most, and it is small: the reader can see the model the site has of them, and change it.

A persona as a graph: the persona in the middle, its topics around it sized by weight, the pages behind each topic, and the citations between them. Filled dots were read, green ones kept, hollow ones came with the preset and are still unread.

## Personas are focus

One persona is not enough, and that is the second thing I learned building [The Cyber Boardroom](https://thecyberboardroom.com): once users try personalisation, they do not want one persona, they want several. I read about cyber security for one reason and about AI development for another, and the version of me that reads one is not interested in the other's picks. A persona is a way to manage focus. The more a reader can do with it, the more attached they become to it.

So you can have as many as you like. Each has its own picks, its own graph, and its own list of articles kept in or put out. What you read while a persona is active counts towards it; your default persona, built from everything you read, keeps counting everything. And so that nobody starts from nothing, there are five ready-made personas built from what this site has published:

| Persona | Starts from | For |
|---|---|---|
| Morgan, the founder | Strategy, pricing, security sized to the startup | Building a company: what to build, what to charge, what to secure and when to stop |
| Rowan, the journalist | News history, story vaults, local evidence | Where news came from, where it could go, and what publishers are owed by the machines that read them |
| Sam, the security lead | Insider risk, blast radius, identity, incidents | Agents as insiders, and the controls that hold when a model does not |
| Kai, the AI builder | Claude Code sessions, hooks, memory, behaviour policies | Making agents work day to day |
| Jordan, the board member | Business models, due diligence, risk acceptance, governance | How AI is changing the business, and how to govern it |

Each one starts from seven or eight articles and a few topics, and becomes yours as you read and curate. The names are made up and you can change them; [your personas](../account/personas.md) is where.

Your personas: the default one built from all your reading, the ones you added, and the five starting points not yet added.

## The iPad problem

I opened the site on my iPad this morning, and it worked well. The balance was in the top bar, the reading account was there, and the front page made sense. Then I thought about opening it on my laptop, and realised I would be a stranger there: a new balance, no history, no persona.

The first article on the meter called that a cheat: open a private window and you start again with £5.00. [Who will game the reading meter](../articles/who-will-game-the-reading-meter.md) explained why that does not matter, because the reader who does it was not going to pay anyway. But it is more interesting than that. The more a persona knows, the worse it is to lose it. Two devices, two strangers, is not a security gap; it is a bad user experience, and it gets worse exactly as the product gets better.

Today the only fix is manual: export your account as JSON on one device and restore it on the other, from [your reading account](../account/index.md#restore). It works, and it is clumsy on purpose, because it shows what is missing.

## What people will pay for

A persona that follows you. To your phone, to your laptop, and to your agent: when I connect to this site through Claude, the agent should read as my security persona when I am working on security, not as a stranger and not as everyone. That is the value proposition, and it is tight: people do not pay because they care about the site, and they should not feel guilty if they don't. They pay because they want to keep their persona, and to have it wherever they read.

The privacy angle does not go away when that happens; it is what makes it possible. The persona lives in the reader's browser today and nowhere else. When it moves, it moves into an [encrypted vault](../articles/what-sgit-is.md) whose key only the reader holds, so the site that stores it cannot read it, and the reader can take it elsewhere. The site pays for the storage it cannot read; the reader pays for the persona that lives in it.

## The question after that

If the persona is worth keeping, the next question is whether there is enough worth reading in it. There are about sixty articles on this site today, across six topics, and the five starting personas were chosen so that each has a real starting shelf. The better the graph of each article becomes, the better every persona built on it becomes, which is why the work on extracting more from each article, more facts, more connections, more ways to read it, matters to this as much as to the articles themselves. [One article, five readers](../articles/one-article-five-readers.md) is the first of it: a librarian, a cartographer, a historian, an explainer and a storyteller sent through each article after it is written, with every item anchored to its sentence.

## What we expect, written down now

These are added to the anchors in [going live with the reading meter](../articles/going-live-with-the-reading-meter.md), after its five were written and before the first payment. They cannot be tested until a persona can follow a reader between devices; they are here so that we cannot pretend later that we expected something else.

| # | Hypothesis | Proved wrong if |
|---|---|---|
| H6 | When a persona can follow a reader between devices for a price, more readers pay for that than top up the meter for reading alone | Fewer pay for the persona than for reading |
| H7 | Readers who have more than one persona pay more often than readers who have one | No difference, among readers who tell us |
