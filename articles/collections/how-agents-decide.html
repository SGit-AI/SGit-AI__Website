How agents decide, and when they stop

What a real decision needs, who enforces each rule, and why an agent that can go anywhere has no reason to stop: six articles on deciding, for people and agents alike.

These six were written over two days and none of them sets out to be a series. Read together they make one argument: deciding well needs more than a yes or a no, and stopping well needs a perspective that has to be designed in, for agents even more than for people.

Agency is not a yes is the frame: seven things a decider needs, from options beyond yes to authority over the system that produced the request, and a scale on which the weakest of them caps the decision. Where is the why? is the same problem met in a permission prompt with no reason on it.

Knowing when to stop starts from people: security champions who had automated away whole classes of bug, then found themselves debating whether GUIDs were random enough. Agents have it worse, because their range is the point. Who are you protecting against? gives one way to stop: name the attacker before deciding how secure to be. Every mistake added a rule shows what happens without one: each mistake adds a rule, the rules become the complexity, and the way back is shipping small components instead.

Hope or enforcement is where the argument is tested: one customer service agent, three designs, and a count of which rules a model is only hoped to keep. The desk's thread note pulls out the line each of them shares.
